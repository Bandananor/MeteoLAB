A guide for those who have found their feet

Experiments and recipes

Ready-made experiments: what to set, how long to wait and where to look to see a particular phenomenon. Where the named sliders and buttons are — in the “Where to find…” table; what the words mean — in the “Meteorology from zero” guide.

Time in the recipes

Model time is given everywhere — the T+ clock (in the top line on a computer, large at the top on a phone). Each start differs a little (small random perturbations at the ground), so the minutes are approximate. To repeat exactly the same run — “Link to the run”.

0How to set up an experiment

  1. Start from the nearest scenario (the buttons in “Scenarios”).
  2. Change one slider at a time — otherwise you cannot tell what worked. Sliders marked ↻ restart the run.
  3. Before the run look at the numbers under the scenario’s description (CAPE, CIN, shear, SRH) — they hint at what will grow.
  4. Save a “Link to the run” or a file before changing things — you can come back to a successful experiment.

1Supercell

What you need: instability (CAPE from ~1500 J/kg), strong 0–6 km shear (from ~20 m/s), a wind veering with height.

  1. The “Classic supercell” scenario. In it the surface wind is 8 m/s from the south-east (150°), by 1 km 17 m/s from the south, by 6 km 26 m/s from the west-south-west.
  2. Wait: a cloud by minute 10, an anvil by minute 15, the mesocyclone column on the view by minutes 20–25; “Storm mode” on the right switches to a supercell, and the “mesocyclone” badge appears once the rotation has held for 10 minutes.
  3. Where to look: the “Rotation (UH)” tab; radar (R) → the SRV product — a green–red pair; radar → Z at a low tilt — a hook on the south-west side; on the right — “Rotation UH 2–5 km” above 400.
  4. Experiment: lower “Wind at 6 km” to 12 m/s (the “Wind profile” group) — the shear becomes weak, and instead of a supercell there is an ordinary storm that quickly rains itself out.

2Split into right and left movers

  1. The “WK supercell” scenario — the textbooks’ classic experiment (Weisman–Klemp).
  2. After 30–50 minutes the storm splits in two: the right mover goes to the right of the wind and rotates anticlockwise, the left one to the left and rotates clockwise.
  3. Where to look: the “Cells” list on the right — two numbered cells (the rotating ones have an orange dot); the “Rotation (UH)” tab — spots of opposite sign; the “Zones” instrument — the red and blue lines show where they will go.
  4. Experiment: take the “Classic supercell” and on the hodograph (right, “Wind: hodograph and shear”) straighten the whole hodograph — put all the circles (0.5, 1, 3 km) on the straight line between 0 and 6 km: if you straighten only the lower ones, the wind above 1 km stays turned. Without the turning of the wind both cells become nearly alike.

3Low-level mesocyclone — signs of tornado danger

The model does not show the tornado itself: it is dozens of times smaller than the grid (1.2 km). But it shows what forecasters judge the danger by: the rotation of the rising air right near the ground.

  1. The “Classic supercell” or “HSLC” scenario.
  2. Strengthen the twist near the ground: in “Wind profile” set “Wind at 0.5 km” to 18–20 m/s and “Wind at 1 km” to 22–25 m/s without changing the directions. SRH 0–1 km (right, “Instability indices”) grows.
  3. Lower the cloud base: “Humidity by layer” → “Surface” to 75–85 % — STP grows.
  4. Wait 30–40 minutes.
  5. Where to look: right → “All the storm’s values” → “Tornadogenesis signs” — the model tells the level and which conditions are met; below — “Low-level rotation (UH 0–1 / 0–3 km)” and the caption under the chart “At what height the updraught rotates” — it will say “a low-level mesocyclone: the updraught rotates right at the ground”. The “Rotation (UH)” tab → in the field panel (on a phone — “Settings ▾”) the layer “0–1 km: rotation at the ground”.
  6. What will not happen: the tornado itself. It is hundreds of metres wide and the model’s grid ~1 km: the model shows only the signs a forecaster warns by. And “a tornadic environment” is not a tornado yet: in nature few storms in such an environment produce one.

4Multicell storm

  1. The “Multicell storm” scenario.
  2. Each cell lives 20–30 minutes, but new ones grow next to it at the edge of the cold outflow.
  3. Where to look: the “Cold pool” tab — the spreading cold air; new clouds rise at its edge; the “Cells” list — numbers appear and disappear; the “Storm history” chart — the updraught pulses.
  4. Experiment: a double click on the ground ahead of the outflow’s edge — your own thermal; see whether it grows faster than far away.

5Microburst

  1. The “Microburst” scenario: hot and dry low down, moist at mid-levels.
  2. The downpour from a high base evaporates in the dry air, the air cools sharply and falls.
  3. Where to look: “Surface gust” on the right; the “Vertical motion” tab — a blue core down to the ground; put a Station under the storm — the meteogram shows a sharp gust and a temperature drop.
  4. Experiment: “Humidity by layer” → “Surface” and “1–3 km” higher (50–60 %) — the gust gets weaker.

6The cap and its breaking

  1. The “Summer day” scenario. “Temperature profile” → “Cap (inversion)” — raise it to 2–3 K. The CIN under the description grows.
  2. The thermal does not break the cap: only flat clouds or nothing — that is a result too.
  3. Now put several thermals side by side with double clicks or raise the “Surface temperature” — see at what CIN the storm still breaks through.
  4. The “Loaded gun” scenario — the cap holds until midday, then the storm bursts out strong at once. Raise the acceleration: it starts around 15:00 by the model’s sun.

7A storm from the sun, no thermal

  1. The “Diurnal cycle” scenario: starts at 6 in the morning, a night inversion, no starting thermal.
  2. Set the acceleration to ×60 or more (the “Computation” group; on a phone — “Speed”). Under the slider you see how much the computer actually manages.
  3. Where to look: Skew-T — how the sun “eats” the morning inversion (the dashes are the initial profile); the “Storm history” chart — CAPE grows towards noon; around noon cumulus appear, then the first storms.

8A storm in a zone of moist air

  1. The “Classic supercell” scenario. Open the “Zones” instrument (Z; on a phone — “Instruments” → “Zones”).
  2. On the map — the thermal’s circle and the path lines: the red one is where the supercell will go. Find the 20′ mark on the red line and a little beyond.
  3. “Draw a zone” → click the 4 corners of a square some 15–20 km around that place → “Done”.
  4. The “Surface moisture” slider → +3 g/kg. On the right it shows CAPE in the zone and around it.
  5. “Apply — restart the run”, close the screen (Esc). The zone shows on the ground in a greenish tint.
  6. When the storm enters the zone (by minutes 35–45), it strengthens: the updraught and the downpour are noticeably higher. For comparison repeat with a −4 g/kg zone — the storm weakens.

9A storm of a real day

  1. Open the University of Wyoming site, choose a station (for example a US one on the Plains in May, or 27612 — Moscow), a date, the 12 or 00 UTC time and “Text: List”.
  2. Select the whole page (Ctrl+A), copy it (Ctrl+C).
  3. In the simulator — “Real sounding…” under the scenarios (on a phone — in the “Scenarios” sheet) → paste the text into the window.
  4. Before the run you see the profile, CAPE, the shear and what was fixed in the data. Choose the thermal strength and press “Run with this sounding”.
  5. The model shows what storm would grow in the atmosphere of that day (without fronts and terrain — one environment for the whole domain; add unevenness with zones).

10Lightning and the lightning jump

  1. The “Classic supercell” scenario, acceleration ×16.
  2. The first lightning — by minutes 18–20. The “Lightning” toggle on the view shows the flashes’ channels.
  3. The “Lightning” instrument (L): on the chart below — the rate rising and a ▼ “jump” triangle at a sharp rise (around minute 23). A click on a mark on the map — the flash’s card.
  4. Experiment: compare with the “Summer day” — less lightning, and it starts later: a weaker updraught means less graupel and fewer collisions.

11Compare two experiments

  1. Run an experiment up to an interesting moment (for example, minute 20).
  2. “Go back to minute…” → choose minute 15: the run goes on from there again.
  3. Before going on, change a setting without the ↻ mark (time of day, sun, soil moisture, turbulence) — and compare what turned out differently.
  4. To compare experiments with different atmospheres — make a “Link to the run” for each and open them in two browser tabs.

12Cold front

  1. The “Cold front” scenario: moist warm air under a weak “cap”, no thermal. A cold front comes from the west — the blue line on the ground.
  2. Wait 35–50 minutes (acceleration ×16 or more): the front’s head lifts the warm air, breaks the cap, and storms rise along the front.
  3. Where to look: the “Temperature” tab — the cold wedge at the ground; “Vertical motion” — the lift over the front’s nose; the “Zones” instrument — the front’s symbols and where it will be in 20 and 40 minutes.
  4. Experiment 1: “Zones” → “Front” → “None” → “Apply”: the same environment without a front — nothing grows in an hour.
  5. Experiment 2: make the front weaker (contrast 4 K, depth 1 km) — storms come later or not at all: only a strong enough wedge breaks the cap.
  6. Experiment 3: “Warm” — warm air flows up over a gentle wedge of cold air: clouds grow above the cold layer, not at the ground.

13One day — three outcomes

  1. The “Upper trough” situation (under the scenarios, “Synoptic situations”). Acceleration ×30 or more. Follow the story under the description: by 11:30 the sun brings storms.
  2. Experiment 1 — an ordinary day: on the left, the “Advection: the environment changes” group → “Remove all”. The same storms by 11:30, but by 13:00 everything dies down: the outflow has filled the model’s whole small domain, and no new cold arrives from above. In nature new storms would grow on the outflow’s edges until evening — this is a limit of the 48 × 36 km domain, not a property of an “ordinary day”.
  3. Experiment 2 — a day without storms: the “Warm air above the layer: no storms” situation. The same air, but at 2–3.5 km it warms by 3 K: the cumulus hit the cap. The CAPE/CIN chart in the “Advection” group shows the CIN growing.
  4. Experiment 3 — below the layer: in the same situation’s change set the layer to 1–2.5 km. Restart — and there will be more storms: in the daytime the mixed layer grows to ~2 km and the warm air heats all of it, like the sun.
  5. Where to look: Skew-T (S) — the profile in the model; on the right, “Storm history” — CAPE and CIN over time.
