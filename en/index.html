02

What’s inside

  1. A real 3D model

    The equations of motion, pressure, buoyancy, cloud, rain, snow, graupel and hail on a ~1 km grid. Not an animation — every frame is computed.

  2. Radar, Skew-T, station

    Three instruments on their own screens: a dual-polarization Doppler radar with cross-sections and mesocyclones, a thermodynamic diagram, a meteogram.

  3. The day from dawn

    The sun heats the ground, eats the night inversion — and in the afternoon the first storms grow out of the heating.

  4. Works on a phone

    3D on the whole screen, large numbers, a panel for the thumb.

  5. A link and a run file

    A link opens the same experiment with the same settings. A run can be saved to a file and continued from the same minute.

  6. Checked against the literature

    Benchmark tests: the Bryan–Fritsch warm bubble, the Straka cold bubble; the parcel — against MetPy; calibration by the Weisman–Klemp experiment.

03

For teaching

An experiment opens from a link with the same settings — hand it out to a class or show it on a projector in the light theme.

Task 1

Where did the rain go?

“Summer day” with a moist and a dry middle

Note the rain total after 40 minutes, then make the middle layer dry (3–7 km humidity — 15 %) and repeat. How did the rain and the cold outflow change? Use the “What is happening” text and the humidity by layer to explain where the water went. The whole class gets the same numbers from the link to the run.

20 min · ages 14–15
Task 2

Where rotation comes from

“Multicell storm” and “Classic supercell”

Both experiments have vortices, but only one has a mesocyclone. Compare the hodographs and the 0–6 km wind shear, find the mesocyclone mark on the radar.

30 min · ages 16–17
Task 3

When will the storm start

“Diurnal cycle”

Follow on the Skew-T how the sun eats the night inversion. Note the time of the first cumulus and the first storm, compare with a forecast from the level of free convection.

45 min · club, university
